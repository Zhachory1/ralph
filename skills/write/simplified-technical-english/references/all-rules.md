## Section 1 — Words

**1.1** Use only words that are (a) approved in the dictionary, (b) technical nouns, or (c) technical verbs. A technical noun/verb names a specific concept in a subject field (e.g. "engine," "ream") and is allowed even if not in the dictionary, provided it fits an approved category (see 1.5 and 1.12).

**1.2** Use an approved word only as its specified part of speech. If a word is approved as a noun, do not use it as a verb, and vice versa.

**1.3** Use an approved word only with its approved meaning. Many common words have one narrow approved sense; other senses are not STE.

**1.4** Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives (the forms listed in the dictionary).

**1.5** You may use any word you can place in an approved **technical-noun category**. The 22 categories: 1) official parts information; 2) vehicles/machines and locations on them; 3) tools and support equipment; 4) materials, consumables, waste; 5) facilities, infrastructure, logistics; 6) systems, components, circuits and their parts/functions; 7) math/science/engineering terms and formulas; 8) navigation and geographic terms; 9) numbers, units, time and their symbols; 10) quoted text; 11) roles, individuals, groups, organizations, geopolitical entities; 12) parts of the body; 13) personal effects, food, drink; 14) medical terms; 15) official documents, standards, guidelines; 16) environmental and operational conditions; 17) colors; 18) damage terms; 19) computing/ICT; 20) civil and military operations; 21) law and regulations; 22) animals, plants, other life forms.

**1.6** Use a word that is not in the dictionary only when it is a technical noun or a technical verb (i.e. it fits a category in 1.5 or 1.12).

**1.7** Do not use a technical noun as a verb (do not "verb" a part name).

**1.8** Use technical nouns that are approved in your company, industry, or subject field — prefer the term in the official glossary/terminology database.

**1.9** When you must choose a technical noun, pick one that is short and easy to understand.

**1.10** Do not use regional words, slang, or jargon as technical nouns.

**1.11** Do not use different technical nouns for the same item. One item = one name, everywhere.

**1.12** You may use any verb you can place in an approved **technical-verb category**: 1) manufacturing processes; 2) computer processes and applications; 3) instructions/information for a subject field; 4) law and regulations.

**1.13** Do not use a technical verb as a noun.

**1.14** Use American English spelling unless an official directive says otherwise.

## Section 2 — Multi-word nouns

**2.1** Write multi-word noun clusters of no more than three words. Long noun strings (e.g. "runway surface friction measurement device") are ambiguous; break them up with prepositions or a full description.

**2.2** When a technical noun genuinely has more than three words, write it in full and do not add more modifiers in front of it.

## Section 3 — Verbs

**3.1** Use only the verb forms given in the dictionary.

**3.2** Use only these forms/tenses: infinitive, imperative (command), simple present, simple past, simple future, and the past participle used as an adjective. **Not allowed:** present perfect (has done), past perfect (had done), present/past progressive (is/was doing), and any other complex/compound verb construction.

**3.3** Use the past participle as an adjective to show a condition ("the disassembled unit," "the unit is disassembled"). This is not passive voice. Only use participles that are approved.

**3.4** Do not use auxiliary verbs to build complex constructions (no "have" + participle, etc.).

**3.5** Use the "-ing" form only as a technical noun or as a modifier in a technical name — never as the main verb of a sentence.

**3.6** Use the active voice. Passive voice is permitted only in descriptive writing, and only when the agent is unknown. Test for passive by asking "by whom or by what?" — if the sentence answers that (often via "by ..."), rewrite it active.

**3.7** Describe an action with an approved verb, not with a noun or other part of speech ("Inspect the seal," not "Do an inspection of the seal").

## Section 4 — Sentences

**4.1** Write short, clear sentences (see 5.1 and 6.3 for the numeric limits by text type).

**4.2** Do not omit words or use contractions to make sentences shorter. Keep articles, subjects, and function words. Shorten by removing ideas, not grammar.

**4.3** Use a vertical list for complex text — when one sentence would otherwise carry many conditions, parts, or steps, break it into a bulleted or numbered list.

**4.4** Use connecting words and phrases (and, or, then, because, after, before, if) to join related sentences and show logical relationships.

**4.5** When applicable, use an article (the, a, an) or a demonstrative adjective (this, these) before a noun. Do not write in a telegraphic, article-dropping style.

## Section 5 — Procedural writing (instructions)

**5.1** Write short sentences: **maximum 20 words** per sentence.

**5.2** Write only one instruction per sentence — unless two or more actions happen at the same time, which may share a sentence.

**5.3** Write instructions in the imperative (command) form: start with the verb.

**5.4** When there is a condition the reader must know first, start the instruction with that condition, then give the command.

**5.5** Write notes to give information only, never instructions. Put required actions in the procedure steps, not in a note.

## Section 6 — Descriptive writing

**6.1** Give information gradually — build up from what the reader already knows.

**6.2** Use key words and key phrases to give the text a logical structure.

**6.3** Write short sentences: **maximum 25 words** per sentence.

**6.4** Use paragraphs to group related information.

**6.5** Each paragraph has only one topic.

**6.6** No paragraph has more than six sentences.

## Section 7 — Safety instructions (warnings, cautions, notes)

**7.1** Use the correct signal word (e.g. "Warning," "Caution") to identify the level of the hazard. (Warning = risk of injury/death; Caution = risk of damage — follow your document's convention.)

**7.2** Start a safety instruction with a clear, accurate command or condition.

**7.3** Then give an explanation that shows the risk or the possible result. Place the warning/caution before the step it applies to.

## Section 8 — Punctuation and word count

**8.1** You may use all standard English punctuation except the **semicolon (;)**. Replace a semicolon with two sentences or a vertical list.

**8.2** Use hyphens to connect words that are directly related (per the dictionary's guidance).

**8.3** You may use parentheses (e.g. for item numbers/callouts and short clarifications).

**8.4** In a vertical list, a colon (:) acts like a period for word-count purposes and introduces the list.

**8.5** Text inside parentheses counts as one word in that sentence.

**8.6** Count each of these as **one word**: numbers; a number with its unit of measurement; abbreviations; alphanumeric identifiers; quoted text; titles/headings/placards/labels; and proper nouns of individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities.

**8.7** A hyphenated word counts as one word.

## Section 9 — Writing practices

**9.1** If a word-for-word replacement does not work, use a different sentence construction to keep the meaning while staying in STE.

**9.2** Use each approved word correctly (right sense, right part of speech).

**9.3** When you put two words together, do not create phrasal verbs (avoid "set up," "carry out," "put on" as verbs — use a single approved verb such as "assemble," "do," "install").

**9.4** Choose terminology and wording with a consistent style throughout the document.
